Model validation#

How well does a TEXAS calibration fit, and how do we know the generalized-logistic Bayesian model improves on existing TEX₈₆ calibrations? This page summarizes the validation reported in the manuscript (Rattanasriampaipong et al., 2026, Paleoceanography and Paleoclimatology); the analysis itself, with every figure, is notebooks/manuscripts/SI_code02_t0shift_TEXAS_analysis.ipynb. Every number below is quoted from the revised manuscript (under review) and can be recomputed from the archived posteriors without refitting.

Validation rests on four complementary checks:

  1. Forward calibration performance — how accurately the curve reproduces the observed proxy (R², RMSE in Scaled RI units).

  2. Residual diagnostics — whether any structure remains in the residuals, against temperature and on a map.

  3. Inverse reconstruction skill — how well the calibration recovers modern SST from the coretop proxy, globally and by ocean basin, against BAYSPAR and the linear and exponential TEX₈₆ calibrations.

  4. Sensitivity — to the proxy definition and to the sampler budget.

All of the coretop metrics are in-sample: they are computed on the gridded compilation the calibration was estimated from (1,518 cells), and the manuscript says so. The culture and mesocosm data constrain the warm end that the coretops never reach.

Both γ coefficients are resolved away from zero with essentially full posterior probability. σ_crtp is pure process noise: the per-site analytical error (σ_meas = 0.03, the TEX₈₆ interlaboratory reproducibility) is carried separately and combined in quadrature, so the implied total spread √(0.03² + 0.039²) ≈ 0.049 matches the realized RMSE of 0.051. The prior scale for σ_crtp is set by the thermal-only fit’s R² (≈ 0.75) — this is the only place that quantity enters the model.

You can reproduce a prediction-versus-observation comparison for any cached posterior with the public API:

import numpy as np
from TEXAS import predict_proxy_from_T

pred = predict_proxy_from_T(
    temperatures=coretop_df["SST"].values,
    posterior="tx.GHPU.sst.sri03.p0",
)
rmse = np.sqrt(np.mean((coretop_df["scaledRI_cren3"] - pred["p50"]) ** 2))

2. Residual diagnostics#

A lower RMSE is necessary but not sufficient: a good calibration should leave no systematic structure in its residuals.

  • Against temperature (Fig. 5d–f). The linear and exponential fits show pronounced structure at the warm end, where the proxy saturates; the sigmoid’s residuals show none across the full range.

  • Spatially (Fig. 8, Figs. S13, S14, S16). Residuals are kriged onto a map with TEXAS.plotting.plot_residual_maps. The linear and TEX₈₆ᴴ calibrations underpredict in polar waters and the Red Sea; BAYSPAR removes most of that but keeps an Arctic residual, because coretops above 70°N were excluded from its training set. The TEXAS univariate model already homogenizes the high latitudes; the nonthermal predictors then remove the Mediterranean and Red Sea warm biases (Red Sea inverse RMSE 6.9 → 2.2 °C). Fig. S16 shows which predictor acts where: G₂/₃ in the tropical Atlantic and subtropical Pacific gyres, NO₃ in the Red Sea and Mediterranean. Kriging six panels takes minutes, so the grids are cached under data/cache/TEXAS_kriged_grids_cache/; pass recompute='auto' to build a missing grid or recompute=True to re-krige.

The attenuation-bias check behind the errors-in-variables treatment (OLS versus ODR slopes of the residuals against each predictor; Text S2, Fig. S11) is in SI_code01_t0shift_variance_partitioning.ipynb.

Two things to read from that table:

  • Adding predictors lowers forward error but not inverse error. Variance the temperature-only model assigns to T is redistributed among T, G₂/₃ and NO₃, and the predictors’ own measurement uncertainty propagates into the inverse. The full model’s global RMSE equals BAYSPAR’s and stays below the linear and exponential calibrations.

  • The posterior width tells the opposite story. The median 1σ half-width falls from 5.1 °C (temperature only) to 3.8 °C (full model); excluding sites poleward of 60° brings TEXAS and BAYSPAR to the same 3.1 °C (Fig. 9). The remaining high-latitude width is the sigmoid’s lower asymptote: with σ_meas = 0.03, inverse uncertainty exceeds ±5 °C below 7.1 °C and above 40.9 °C (Fig. 7d).

By ocean basin (Fig. S17; the 21 Natural Earth regions with ≥ 20 cells, 1,286 of 1,518 sites): a TEXAS configuration has the lowest regional RMSE in 12 of 21 regions, BAYSPAR in 7, the exponential calibration in 2. The full TEXAS model’s regional RMSE spans 2.1–5.7 °C against 1.4–9.1 °C for BAYSPAR, 2.1–9.7 °C linear and 1.3–12.7 °C exponential, and the standard deviation of regional RMSE across the 21 regions (1.1–1.2 °C for the three TEXAS configurations) is about half BAYSPAR’s (2.2 °C). That comparison is conservative for TEXAS, which uses one global calibration where BAYSPAR’s coefficients vary by region.

Downcore applications with full posterior uncertainty are in SI_code03_paleo_showcases.ipynb (manuscript Section 8).

4. Sensitivity#

  • Proxy definition (Fig. S12). Refitting with crenarchaeol counted as 4 or 5 rings instead of 3 moves none of the four response parameters outside its 68 % interval (T₀ = 34.8, 35.4, 35.7 °C) and leaves σ_crtp at 0.0391; R²_fwd falls monotonically from 0.803 to 0.785 and 0.772, which is why Scaled RI₀₋₃ is adopted. γ_{G₂/₃} is the sensitive coefficient (+20.5 % across conventions), so users of another ring convention should refit rather than transfer it.

  • Sampler budget (Text S3, Figs. S18–S19; the sampler budget page). Every forward budget that clears the convergence gates agrees with a long reference run to within 0.07 SD for every parameter.
